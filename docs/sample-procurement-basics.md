# Sample procurement basics

A short orientation for anyone newer to buying sample — and the mental model
this kit's agents are built on. Terms are defined in the
[glossary](glossary.md).

## The lifecycle

Every sample purchase moves through roughly the same six stages. The kit has
one agent per stage, plus a cross-cutting specialist (the Incentive Advisor)
consulted alongside stages 2–4:

| Stage | What happens | Agent |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Brief | Turn the research ask into a precise spec: audience, quals, market, n, LOI, quotas, timeline | Brief Builder |
| 2. Feasibility gut-check | Ask "can this actually be fielded?" before suppliers do | Feasibility Checker |
| 3. RFP | Send suppliers a request they can quote accurately against | RFP Drafter |
| 4. Bids | Compare quotes on assumptions, not just price | Bid Comparator |
| 5. Pre-field | Review the questionnaire for things that will hurt fielding | Questionnaire Risk Reviewer |
| 6. In field | Watch the early signals; act before problems compound | Fielding Risk Advisor |

## What actually drives cost

CPI isn't arbitrary. It moves with:

- **IR** — the dominant driver. Halving IR roughly doubles the number of
  people a supplier must send into your survey.
- **LOI** — longer surveys pay respondents more and lose more of them.
- **Audience rarity** — niche B2B titles, HCP specialties, and low-incidence
  conditions cost multiples of gen-pop.
- **Market** — small or less-panelled countries cost more and field slower.
- **Quotas** — especially interlocked ones and cells that fight natural panel
  composition (young men, high earners, rural).
- **Timeline** — speed costs money; a too-short field window can make an
  otherwise easy project infeasible.

This kit's agents discuss these drivers and their direction. They never
predict a specific CPI — that number belongs to suppliers quoting against a
real spec at a real moment in time.

## Why feasibility is always uncertain

Feasibility depends on things nobody fully controls: actual IR (vs. assumed),
panel composition on the days you field, competing surveys chasing the same
people, seasonality, and how your screener behaves in the wild. Suppliers with
excellent tools still miss. That's why this kit talks about feasibility
**risk** — Low / Medium / High, with reasons — and never certainty.

## The classic failure modes

Most fielding disasters trace back to a handful of avoidable causes:

1. **Optimistic IR.** The client "feels like" 20% of parents have a child
   with a food allergy; reality is single digits. Everything downstream —
   price, timeline, feasibility — was built on the wrong number.
2. **The brief hid a constraint.** Quotas, market splits, or device
   requirements surfaced after bids were in. Suppliers re-quote; timelines
   slip.
3. **Bids compared on price alone.** The cheapest bid assumed the highest IR
   and included an IR adjustment clause. It was never actually the cheapest.
4. **The screener telegraphed the quals.** "Do you have a child with a food
   allergy?" as question one invites yes-saying and fraud. IR looks great;
   data is garbage.
5. **Nobody watched the first 48 hours.** Soft-launch signals (real IR, real
   LOI, drop-off) were available and ignored; by day 5 the project was
   unrecoverable inside the original window.
6. **The last quota cells were an afterthought.** The topline looked on
   track while two interlocked cells sat empty — and those cells were the
   whole reason for the quota scheme.

Each agent in this kit exists to catch one or more of these before they cost
you money, time, or credibility.
