# Practitioner benchmarks — field-tested starting values

The agents ship with general heuristics. This page is different: it's a set
of starting values and mechanics from the author's fourteen years selling
sample on the supplier side, mostly B2B. Paste any section — or the whole
page — into an agent's **"Your benchmarks"** section and it will reason from
these instead of its generic rules of thumb.

Honesty first: these are calibration priors, not guarantees. They were
learned selling B2B (especially senior and financial-services audiences).
The *mechanics* — funnels, overlap, saturation, incentive logic — hold
broadly across consumer, B2B, and healthcare; the *magnitudes* shift by
audience, so treat every number as a starting point your own projects
should overwrite. Values as of early 2026.

## 1. Feasibility is a funnel, and it starts from the universe

Any feasibility claim can be sanity-checked top-down through five layers:

```
How many such people exist (public data: BLS, registries, industry bodies)
  × how many match the targeting (seniority, geography, firm type)
  × how many pass the in-survey screeners
  × how many any source can actually REACH
  × how many of those actually respond and complete
= plausible completes
```

Two rules make this useful:

- **Anchor on public data, not panel claims.** Start from what exists
  (registries, labor statistics), not from what a supplier says they have.
  A quote that implies more qualified completes than the universe contains
  is impossible arithmetic, and it happens more often than you'd think.
- **Bands, not points.** Carry a low–high range through every layer. A
  point estimate of completes is false confidence wearing a decimal.

## 2. Panels overlap: 1 + 1 ≠ 2

Adding a second panel does not double reach. The same professionals —
especially senior ones — are visible to many panels, so combined reach is
far closer to "the best single source, plus a modest lift" than to a sum.
Rules of thumb:

- The rarer and more senior the audience, the harder the overlap cap
  bites. For very niche senior audiences, assume stacking panels adds
  little beyond the best single panel.
- The exception: a genuine boutique/specialty panel with proprietary
  recruitment can add real incremental reach. Ask what makes their pool
  distinct, not how big it is.
- The universe is a hard ceiling no amount of stacking gets past.
- The pre-award question this arms: *"Which panel partners, and how do you
  de-duplicate across them?"* — if the answer is vague, assume the overlap
  is being double-counted in the feasibility claim.

## 3. Timeline: panel saturates, other sources climb

- **Panel response is front-loaded.** Most panel completes land in the
  first ~2 weeks. Extending a panel-only field past ~4 weeks buys little —
  the reachable pool is exhausted, not shy. The fix for a panel-only
  shortfall is **add sources, not weeks** — assuming the field was fully
  released; a deliberately staged or throttled launch is a different
  story.
- **Active recruitment, expert networks, and phone outreach keep
  climbing** with time — they generate new reachable people each week, so
  longer timelines genuinely help there.
- **The delivery-shape check (use this on every bid):** ask for expected
  completes by week. Given a full launch into open quotas with unchanged
  sourcing, a panel-only study on a ~4-week field typically lands roughly
  two-thirds of its completes in week one. So a flat, linear weekly plan
  from panel-only sourcing earns a follow-up, not an accusation — staged
  releases, quota throttling, and resend waves legitimately flatten the
  curve. Ask about invitation/release cadence first; if the answer is
  "none of those," something non-panel is quietly in the mix.

## 4. LOI decay

Relative to a 15-minute baseline, completion propensity decays roughly
10–15% for every 5 minutes of added length: at 20 minutes expect ~85% of
the baseline rate, at 30 minutes ~55–70%, at 45 minutes roughly half or
less — and senior audiences sit at the steep end. Under ~10 minutes you get
a modest lift. This compounds with everything else, which is why an
"18-minute survey, probably" in a brief is a cost and feasibility event,
not a detail.

## 5. Screener strictness → expected pass rate

Of the people who already match the audience *targeting*, the share that
passes the additional in-survey screeners typically lands in these bands
(note the denominator — targeted entrants, not gen-pop):

| Screener strictness | Typical pass rate |
|---|---|
| Light validation only (role confirm, light quals) | 70–90% |
| Moderate role / workflow fit | 40–70% |
| Strict recent behavior ("in the last 90 days…") | 20–40% |
| Niche exposure + decision authority stacked | 5–25% |

These bands are priors from B2B practice, not benchmarks: a single
behavior qual can pass 5% or 90% depending on the behavior, the source,
and the targeting. Match the regime, keep the denominator honest, and
prefer any observed comparable you have. What the bands are for is
spotting order-of-magnitude mismatches — if a brief's IR assumption
implies "light validation" but the questionnaire on the page is "strict
recent behavior," that's a pre-launch risk to reconcile. Don't derive an
IR from a strictness label.

## 6. Senior B2B response reality

At adequate incentive and timeline, senior B2B panel-invite-to-complete
rates are small: often fractions of a percent to low single digits.
Expert-network sourcing converts far better per contact (curated,
scheduled, paid at consulting rates) — which is why it costs multiples.
The practical read: for senior/niche B2B, headline panel sizes are nearly
meaningless; conversion mechanics decide feasibility.

## 7. Incentives: opportunity-cost logic

An incentive that works is anchored to what the respondent's time is worth,
times a premium for how you're reaching them:

```
time value  = (approximate salary ÷ 2,080 hours) × (LOI minutes ÷ 60)
incentive   ≈ time value × access premium × scarcity adjustments
```

- **Access premium by sourcing:** panel respondents opted in, so the
  premium is modest (roughly 1–3× time value for senior/exec titles, less
  for junior). Actively recruited respondents didn't ask to be contacted —
  expect roughly 2–6× time value. Expert networks price like consulting:
  2–5× with high absolute floors.
- **Floors are real.** A 5-minute survey still needs a meaningful amount to
  move a senior person — think in tens of dollars minimum for executives on
  panel, low hundreds via expert networks. Time-value math alone underprices
  short surveys.
- **Scarcity adjustments:** hard-to-reach functions (legal/compliance,
  procurement, data/security) run meaningfully above average; marketing and
  sales below. Each stacked qualifier (named companies, specific behavior,
  certifications, revenue thresholds) adds roughly 20–50% — the loading
  pays for the longer screener, the verification burden, and the greater
  reluctance of qualified people, NOT for rarity itself (rarity's cost
  lands in CPI — see below). By three or more stacked qualifiers you're
  also beyond what panel profiling reliably targets.
- **Diminishing returns:** doubling the incentive does not double response —
  the curve flattens hard past "adequate" (think +25% response at 2× an
  adequate incentive, saturating entirely somewhere around 1.5× the base
  rate). But *under*-paying costs more than it saves: no-shows, slow
  fields, and the wrong people qualifying are all cheaper to prevent than
  to fix.
- **Incentive ≠ CPI.** The incentive is what the respondent receives; CPI
  adds recruitment, screening, project management, and margin. If a quoted
  CPI is close to or below what the respondent's time is plausibly worth,
  ask what the respondent is actually receiving — data quality lives in
  that gap.
- **Price to the hardest quota cell,** not the blended audience. A study
  that's 90% easy and 10% CISOs is a CISO study, incentive-wise, for
  exactly the cell that will decide whether you close.
- **Low incidence raises CPI, not incentive.** Qualified respondents'
  time isn't worth more because they're rare — but finding them costs
  more. Expect the delta in CPI, and push back if a supplier frames it as
  incentive.

## 8. Seniority-fraud patterns (who's really in the panel?)

- Genuine C-suite executives of 50+ employee companies rarely sit in
  online panels. A panel bid promising hundreds of them at consumer-ish
  CPIs deserves the question, not the benefit of the doubt.
- VPs at 1,000+ employee companies: similar caution, milder.
- "VP" or "CEO" at a sub-10-person company is usually a founder/owner —
  a legitimately reachable and different audience. Screen for which one
  you mean.
- Named-company targeting is generally beyond panel capability — that's
  active-recruitment work.
- Marketplace/self-serve pricing with senior-title targeting mostly buys
  people willing to claim senior titles at low incentives.

## 9. Reading a supplier's feasibility number

Four buckets, each with its move:

1. **Above the universe** — mathematically impossible; ask what universe
   and audience definition they're using.
2. **Above plausible response** (but under the universe) — usually panel
   overlap double-counted, stale profile-based IR, or quiet
   supplementation with other sourcing. Ask: partners + dedup method;
   live-screener vs. profile-based IR; whether anything non-panel is in
   the mix and at what cost.
3. **In band** — reasonable; confirm assumptions (IR base and basis,
   timeline buffer, sourcing mix) before committing.
4. **Well below** — they're being conservative or capacity-constrained.
   Ask what's limiting them; sometimes a longer timeline or different mix
   unlocks it, sometimes their honesty is the most useful data point in
   the whole bid process.
