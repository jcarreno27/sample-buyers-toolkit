# Incentive Advisor — example

Three runs, one per regime the agent asks about first: a B2B study, a
consumer study, and a healthcare-professional study. Each is priced the way
its regime actually works. All names are fictional; the dollar figures
illustrate the method and lean on published consumer-incentive norms, not a
guarantee for your project.

## Run 1 — B2B ITDMs

**The user pasted:** US IT decision-makers, Director+, at 500+ employee
companies, n=150, LOI 20 min, active recruitment under consideration
because two named-vendor usage requirements stack on the role. Hardest
cell: 25 completes from financial-services firms. Flexible-ish timeline.

**The agent returned (abridged):**

```
INCENTIVE RECOMMENDATION — ITDM study
Regime: B2B (opportunity-cost)

THE ANCHOR (arithmetic, challenge any line)
ASSUMPTION: Director+ IT at 500+ employee US companies ≈ $150K/yr
÷ 2,080 ≈ $72/hr × (20 min ÷ 60) ≈ $24 base time value

RECOMMENDED RANGE: $100-$170 per complete
($24 time value × 3-5 access ≈ $72-$120; × ~1.4 qualifier loading
≈ $100-$170 after rounding)
| Tier | Amount | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | $115 | flexible timeline, screener as-is |
| Compressed | $140 | timeline tightens or FS cell drags |
| Premium | $165+ | if named-vendor verification is added |

ADJUSTMENTS APPLIED
- Sourcing (active recruitment): ×3-5 on time value. These people
  didn't ask to be contacted; the premium pays for the interruption
  and identity verification, not vanity.
- Stacked qualifiers (2: named-vendor usage × 2): +~40%. This pays
  for the longer screener, the verification, and the reluctance of
  qualified Directors; the incidence drop itself shows up in your
  CPI, not in the respondent's pocket. A third stacked qualifier would
  also be a sourcing red flag (beyond reliable panel profiling), but
  you're already sourcing around that.
- Function scarcity: IT decision-makers ~average for B2B; no bump.
- Floor: not binding at 20 min (binds on short surveys, where time
  value alone underprices the interruption).

CAUTIONS
- Low end ($100) will underperform for actively recruited Directors:
  it prices the time but not the full interruption. Use it only if the
  screener is light and the timeline is genuinely loose.
- Hardest-cell rule: the 25 FS completes are the project. Price that
  cell at the Compressed tier ($140) even if the rest fields at
  Standard.
- One incentive across Director to C-suite is fine here (narrow span);
  if the client adds CIOs at 5,000+ companies, split the incentive.
- Incentive is not CPI: recruitment, screening, verification, PM, and
  margin stack on top. If a bid's CPI lands near these incentive
  numbers, ask what the respondent actually receives.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS
- Your own fielded incentives for ITDM studies (they beat this model)
- Whether "named-vendor usage" must be verified or just claimed
- The real timeline

ASK YOUR SUPPLIER
1. What does the respondent actually receive, in what form, how fast?
2. If the FS cell stalls, is the fix a cell-level bump, and who funds it?
3. At your proposed incentive, what share of invited Directors do you
   expect to even open the invite? (Their answer's confidence tells
   you plenty.)
```

## Run 2 — consumer (Allergy Parents)

**The user then asked:** "Same audience as my other project: US parents of
kids with a diagnosed food allergy, 15-min survey, fielded on a consumer
panel. What incentive?"

**The agent returned (abridged):**

```
INCENTIVE RECOMMENDATION — Allergy Parents
Regime: consumer (effort-anchored)

THE ANCHOR (basis)
Consumer, ~15 min, standard online panel (habitual panelists).
Length-based, not salary-based.

RECOMMENDED RANGE: on a standard consumer panel the panel sets it (points
or a small gift card, often ~$1-5 for 15 min); ~$15-25 if you recruit
these parents directly (the recruited 15-min band)
| Case | Amount | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Habitual gen-pop panel | ~$1-5 (panel-set) | you buy panel sample; the panel sources and pays, in points or a small gift card |
| Directly recruited | ~$15-25 gift card | you recruit these parents outside a panel |
| Slow-cell bump | small add-on | if the 13-17 cell is dragging (a pacing lever, not an incidence one) |

ADJUSTMENTS APPLIED
- Directly recruited, a 15-min ask sits between the ~$5-15 (10 min) and
  ~$25-50 (20-30 min) recruited-consumer bands, so ~$15-25. Returns flatten
  well before the top of the range, so there's no case for going high on a
  15-min survey. A habitual panel typically sets less than this (points or
  a small gift card) and delivers it for you.
- Low incidence (diagnosed food allergy) is real, but it raises the cost
  to FIND qualified parents (your CPI), not what each parent's 15 minutes
  is worth. Don't inflate the per-person incentive to solve an incidence
  problem.
- Delivery: gift cards are the consumer norm; the form and speed of
  payment often move response more than a few dollars of amount.

CAUTIONS
- Underpaying still selects for junk: points-chasers and yes-sayers are
  the consumer version of title-claimers. Sit at the panel's normal rate
  for this length, not below it.
- Hardest-cell rule: if the 13-17 age band stalls, a modest cell-level
  bump (a larger gift card) is a fair lever, with the same flattening
  returns past adequacy.
- Incentive is not CPI: your CPI adds recruitment, the low-incidence
  screening, PM, and margin on top of what the parent receives.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS
- Your panel's actual published rate for a 15-min consumer survey (that
  beats this range)
- Whether you recruit directly or use an existing panel
- How the age-band cells are pacing

ASK YOUR SUPPLIER
1. What does a parent actually receive, in what form, and how quickly
   after completing?
2. If the 13-17 cell stalls, do you bump that cell, and who funds it?
```

*(These are US starting ranges that lean toward recruited research; the
panel's own norms beat them.)*

## Run 3 — healthcare professional (the one number the agent won't give)

**The user then asked:** "We also want pediatric allergists (physicians) on
a 20-minute survey about treatment. What honorarium?"

**The agent's reply (abridged):**

> Different regime again, and this is the one where the honest answer is to
> not put a number on it. Physician honoraria are held to fair-market
> value, not the opportunity-cost math I use for B2B: you anchor to a
> pediatric-allergist FMV hourly rate and prorate by length, and your
> compliance team's FMV table sets the defensible rate and the cap.
> Specialists like allergists run well above primary care, and KOLs higher
> still. On reporting: a properly blinded market-research survey (the
> sponsor never learns who answered) is generally excluded from US Open
> Payments / Sunshine Act reporting; that obligation usually attaches only
> if the work is unblinded or drifts into advisory or consulting. I'll give
> you the anchor structure and the questions to ask. I won't hand you a
> dollar figure, not because the method is secret (it isn't), but because
> the defensible rate, the cap, and the reportability call belong to your
> compliance team, not to me.

## Why this example matters

Run 1 shows the B2B method in full. Run 2 applies the same discipline to a
consumer audience with real, length-based numbers, and holds the line that
low incidence is a CPI problem, not an incentive one. Run 3 shows the one
regime where withholding a number is the correct move: HCP honoraria belong
to fair-market-value rules and a compliance team, so the agent hands back
the structure, not the figure. Three audiences, one rule: give the most
calibrated answer the regime honestly supports, and no more.
